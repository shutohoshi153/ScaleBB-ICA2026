# §7 Repositioning — Scale BB-D as a Scenario Generator for ICS / IFRS 17

This chapter reads the gap found in §5–§6 through the two evaluation axes set out in §2.3. On the first axis — is the point forecast correct? — the negative conclusion of §5 stands. Economic-value-based evaluation (ICS / IFRS 17 / Solvency II, and in Japan the ESR), however, mainly asks the second: can the model generate a set of scenarios from one base in an internally consistent way? We organize the meaning of the §6 findings for economic-value evaluation into three points (§7.1). We then discuss how the framework implements the scenarios that regulation requires (§7.2), the structural reasons why the baseline methods cannot play the same role (§7.3), the division of roles with the ESG (§7.4), and the direct application to death benefits contingent on specific diseases (§7.5).

---

## 7.1 Reading the findings again — three structural properties

For economic-value evaluation, we can organize the validation results up to §6 into the following three points.

1. Stable direction is the basic condition for consistency between scenarios. As we said in §2.3, the real substance of a regulatory calculation is "generating a set of scenarios that start from the same base". The difference between base and stress must correspond one-to-one with the effect of the stress itself. If the sign of the trend that the model outputs is wrong, then the difference we get by moving the long-term rate $L$ becomes a mixture of "the effect of the shock" and "calibration error". We cannot use such a difference as a basis for capital calculation or disclosure. The backtest in §6 gives this basic condition — the sign of the improvement rate $i^*(x, y)$ is validated in advance — for the six main diseases (for direction-reversal diseases, we handle them by the division of roles in §6.5).

2. The explicit output of improvement rates makes direct implementation of shocks possible. As equation (3.6) shows, future rates are fully rebuilt from the base-year rates and the improvement rate path $i^*(x, y)$. Therefore every scenario definition can be written as an operation on $i^*$. Replacing a small number of interpretable parameters — the long-term rate $L$, the convergence year $P$, and the age taper $\tau(x)$ — is itself scenario generation. Methods that "do not have an improvement rate" do not have this operating surface (§7.3).

3. Two-dimensional smoothing guarantees a consistent age curve in all scenarios. The Phase 1 fit is shared between scenarios. Only Phase 2 (blending and projection) is run again for each scenario. So all scenarios start from the same smoothed age curve and the same base-year rates. The difference between scenarios comes only from the difference in the improvement rate paths. The four requirements listed in §2.3 (keeping the shape of the age curve, preserving relative relations, separating short term and long term, and making many scenarios comparable with each other) are all satisfied at the same time by this structure.

Table 7.1: Scenario types required by regulation and their implementation in this framework

| Scenario type / calculation | Regulatory example | Implementation in this framework |
|---|---|---|
| Base scenario (current estimate, BEL) | Future rates $m(x, y)$ with assumptions re-evaluated at the valuation date | Use the projected rates of equation (3.6) as they are |
| Level shock | ESR: mortality +12.5% (notice Article 56), mortality −20% (longevity, Article 57), incidence rates for morbidity and disability +20% (Articles 59 and 60) | Uniform multiplication of the projected rates (multiply the Phase 2 output by a factor). ICS_C and ESR_M in §8 |
| Trend shock | Change of the future improvement-rate trend (type classification in §2.3; it feeds both the current estimate itself and, as a shock, ORSA, internal management and sensitivity disclosure, §8.5) | Replace $L$ → only a Phase 2 re-projection. UP50 / DN50 / ICS_T in §8 |
| Catastrophe shock | Infectious disease outbreak: mortality rises at once by 1/1,000 (notice Article 95) | Rewrite the first-year $i^*(x, y_0{+}1)$ so that $m(x, y_0{+}1)$ becomes $+0.001$, then re-project |
| Sensitivity disclosure (IFRS 17) | Change in liabilities when key insurance risk assumptions move within a reasonable range | Run a grid of $L \pm 50$bp, $P \pm 5$ years, and $a_0 \pm 5$ years of age |
| ORSA and medium/long-term stress | Quantify narrative scenarios such as worse public health or a stop in medical progress | Reset $L = 0$ or to a negative value (the behavior check for $L \le 0$ is in §6.5) |

The implementation of the level shock row deserves a note, because the notice's Q&A specifies it precisely. For life insurance risk, the prescribed stress is applied by multiplying the rate assumption used in the current estimate by $(1 + X\%)$, for every contract, at every age, over the whole projection period, in every homogeneous risk group where an increase in the rate reduces net assets; the stressed mortality rate is capped at 100%. Longevity risk, morbidity and disability risk, and the level-and-trend part of lapse risk follow the same pattern. For morbidity, the Q&A adds that where the cash flows are modelled with incidence and recovery rates, the stress is applied to the incidence rate only. Our implementation of ICS_C and ESR_M — a uniform multiplication applied to the whole projected rate surface — is therefore the implementation the regulation contemplates, not an approximation of it. The framework's contribution on this row is not the shock itself but the surface it is applied to: a projected surface whose improvement path is explicit and directionally validated, rather than a static table carried forward.

The implementation column of Table 7.1 has one common point. All rows share the same Phase 1 fit. Each scenario is generated only by a Phase 2 re-projection with a changed `ScaleBBConfig` setting (or by multiplying the output by a factor). Phase 1 does not depend on $L$, so the marginal cost of adding a scenario is only one Phase 2 run. Also, with the APC extension (§3.3), cohort shocks — such as extra risk for a specific generation or faster improvement — can be implemented in the same pattern as operations on the cohort effect $\gamma(c)$. Note that this last pattern is a design property of the framework, not a validated result: the backtest of §5–§6 does not cover the APC extension (see the scope note in §3.3, and §10.3 for the corresponding future work).

The difference between a level shock and a trend shock is not just a difference in implementation. It appears as a difference in the age structure of the risk. To preview the numerical example of §8.4: the effect of the regulatory level shock (incidence rate +20%) on the present value of benefits is almost uniform across entry ages (+17 to 19%). The trend shock (zero improvement rate) has a clear age gradient: +31 to 32% for entry at age 30, and +10 to 11% for entry at age 60. The current method, which multiplies a static table by a uniform factor, cannot express a risk with this age structure in principle.

## 7.3 Why the baseline methods cannot play the same role

The baseline methods, which gave the most accurate point forecasts in §5, do not work as scenario generators for structural reasons.

`naive_last` / `mean_3pts` have no concept of an improvement rate. They can only express a multiplication of the level (a level shock). There is no place to define a trend shock — an intervention in the time structure of the rates. As we saw in §6.3, they also carry no direction information (or only by chance). So the very picture of a scenario as "a deviation from a base whose direction has been validated" cannot be built.

`loglin_trend` has an explicit trend, the OLS slope, and as §6.3 shows, its direction information is fairly good. However, it lacks the structure needed for scenario generation in three points. First, it has no time structure. It fixes the slope of the observation window used for estimation (the most recent 15 years) for the whole future. So it cannot express the Phase 2 time structure (equation 3.5) — "continue the observed trend in the short term, and converge to the assumed improvement rate in the long term" — or requirement 3 of §2.3, such as "a trend shock for 5 years only, then normal afterwards". Second, it has no age structure. It is an independent OLS for each age, so it has no age × period interaction. It can shift the slope uniformly, but an age-dependent shock such as "improvement continues for young ages, and slows down for old ages" needs manual settings age by age. It also has no taper at high ages, so the extrapolation can easily diverge. Improvement rates can flip between neighboring ages, and the rate surface after a shock loses its smoothness. A rate curve that is inconsistent across ages cannot be used for product design or as a basis for capital calculation. Third, if we try to fix these problems from outside (manually reversing the slope, adjusting the slope age by age), we give up the assumptions of the estimation method. We fall back to ad-hoc table manipulation, and we lose the §2.3 property that "the difference corresponds one-to-one to the shock".

From this we can say: the family of point forecasters and short-term extrapolators was built to "hit a single future". It does not have the operating surface — an explicit output of improvement rates with validated direction — needed to "span several futures consistently". Whether this operating surface exists or not is the essential difference that the MAPE comparison in §5 could not show.

## 7.4 Contrast with the ESG — a deterministic scenario generator for the disease-risk axis

In insurance practice, the word "scenario generator" makes people think of the ESG (Economic Scenario Generator). So we make the difference in roles clear. The ESG is a device for the economic-risk axes, such as interest rates and equities. It generates thousands of stochastic paths under a probability measure. It supports market-consistent valuation and the calculation of the time value of options and guarantees. In contrast, this framework covers deterministic scenario generation for the disease-risk axis — for medical insurance, disease incidence rates (as a proxy); for death benefits contingent on specific diseases, cause-specific mortality (directly). The insurance risks in ICS / ESR require prescribed deterministic stresses, not stochastic paths (§2.3, §8.1). For the economic axes, a systematic generation device, the ESG, already exists. For the disease-risk axis there is no counterpart. The current practice is to multiply a static assumed rate table by a uniform factor. This proposal fills this gap — assumption practice that cannot express a trend shock — and creates a new device that forms a pair with the ESG on the economic axes.

The two do not compete. They share roles and meet in the projection engine. The ESG supplies the discount rates and market scenarios. This framework supplies the rate tables for each scenario. The liability valuation model consumes both. The demonstration in §8 is calculated exactly in this setup — an ESR-compliant discount rate curve × claim rate tables by scenario. A stochastic extension based on the residual distribution of improvement rates (for example, bootstrap generation from a distribution of $L$) is a natural future extension of this framework, but it is outside the scope of this paper (§10). Nor does adding this generator demand a large infrastructure investment: it sits upstream of the projection engine and outputs exactly the rate-table format that valuation practice already consumes, so it replaces, rather than adds to, the current process for building stress tables. §9.2 analyses that adoption burden in full and sets out a step-by-step adoption path.

## 7.5 Direct application to death benefits contingent on specific diseases — two layers of applicability

Finally, we make the scope of this framework clear, following the double positioning in §3.1.3. For morbidity benefits in medical insurance, cause-specific mortality is a proxy for the incidence rate. The direction validation in §6 should be read as "validation of the methodology" — validation with incidence rates themselves is future work (§10). On the other hand, for products where cause-specific death is the benefit trigger — death benefits for the three major diseases, cancer death insurance, and disease-specific death riders by cause — the cause-specific mortality validated in this study is the assumption itself. We can apply the scenario generator directly, without the proxy caveat. Trend shocks through operations on $L$, and level shocks through the notice factors, feed directly into the sensitivity of the present value of benefits for these products. (In §8 we show that the same rate table, depending on how we read it, supports both regulatory cases: the morbidity stress (ESR_M) and the mortality stress (the level part of ICS_C).)

So the practical scope of this framework has two layers. For medical insurance (incidence-rate products), it works as a proxy application of a validated methodology. For death benefits contingent on specific diseases, it works as a direct application. In the second layer, the direction validation in §6 is itself the validation of that assumption. We do not claim to forecast the case fatality rate (mortality after diagnosis) in either layer (§10).
