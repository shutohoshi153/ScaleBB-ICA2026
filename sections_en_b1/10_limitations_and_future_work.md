# §10 Limitations and Future Work

In this chapter, we organize the limitations of this study into 5 areas. For each area, we describe the related future work. We already mentioned most of these limitations in earlier chapters. Here, we place them in the context of the whole paper. We also give concrete paths for future validation and extension.

---

## 10.1 Proxy nature of the data — Limited scope of application and direct validation with incidence rates

The first limitation is the proxy nature of the input data. This study validated cause-specific mortality rates. For the disease incidence rates of medical insurance (benefits paid on diagnosis), our work is only a proxy validation of the methodology (§3.1.3). Between incidence rates and cause-specific mortality rates, there is a third factor: the case fatality rate (mortality after diagnosis), which changes over time. Because of this, our validation cannot separate differences in level and trend. So the application to incidence-rate products is indirect. It rests on this basis: the methodology was validated on series that share the same age and cohort structure. However, this limitation applies only to incidence-rate products. For death benefits contingent on specific diseases (Novelty B), cause-specific mortality is the assumption itself. So the proxy problem does not appear there (§7.6).

The future task is direct validation with incidence rates. To build incidence rates themselves, the best path uses databases that follow individuals over time. Examples are health insurance society claims data (medical billing records) and health check-up records. With the insured group as the denominator and first disease events by disease as the numerator, we can build incidence rates by year and age. These rates do not depend on medical treatment rates or mortality rates. Then a backtest of the same form as in this paper becomes possible for all diseases. However, these databases are sold as paid big data. At present, the cost is a large barrier for research use.

We also confirm again that the case fatality rate (mortality after diagnosis) is outside the scope of this study. The case fatality rate needs the number of diagnosed cases as its denominator. We cannot get it from cause-specific mortality per total population. Estimating the case fatality trend as cause-specific mortality divided by observed incidence is future work. This study does not claim, in any form, that it can predict the prognosis after diagnosis.

## 10.2 Testability of long-term forecast accuracy — Handle it through scenarios, not by waiting for data

The second limitation is that the long-term effect of the long-term rate $L$ itself is not yet validated. As we said in §6.5, $L$ and $P$ control the direction after the convergence year. Under the default setting ($P = 2035$), no actual data exists yet for that period. The 10-year backtest validated the short- to mid-term direction in the blending interval.

However, this study does not treat this limitation in the frame of "wait for data to build up, then complete the validation". The reasons and the response follow the structure discussed at the end of §6.5. (i) The part we can validate with limited data — the direction of improvement rates — was fixed as the foundation by the validation in this paper (§6). (ii) The long-term assumptions that validation cannot reach are not hidden inside the model as implicit premises. We made them explicit as the interpretable parameters $L$ and $P$. We turned them into objects that we vary as scenarios (§7–§8). (iii) While we wait for actual data to build up, the next structural break — a new infectious disease outbreak, or a shift in medical technology — can enter the data. So a validation that waits for data can never finish, as a matter of principle. Against structural breaks, two defenses work. After the event, regular refitting corrects the direction by itself (the data path in §6.5, already shown with COVID-19). Before the event, we can quantify the risk as stress scenarios, such as $L \le 0$ or a catastrophe shock (Table 7.1). We do not try to remove long-term uncertainty by hitting point forecasts. Instead, we handle uncertainty explicitly on top of the validated direction. This stance fits the requirements of economic-value-based evaluation (§2.3). We believe it is a realistic way to support the reliability of disease-rate assumptions when only limited data is available.

## 10.3 Building a systematic method for disease-specific calibration

The third limitation concerns the disease-specific calibration guidance in §9.1. It is provisional. We induced it from a backtest of 8 diseases × 3 cutoffs. Future work has the following 3 points. First, upgrade the choice of $L$ and $P$ to a systematic selection procedure. It should be based on directional accuracy in pseudo out-of-sample tests with shifted cutoffs. Second, check that the guidance stays stable at finer disease levels, such as ICD mid-level categories, and at finer age levels than 5-year age groups. Third, validate projections that include the cohort effect of the APC extension (§3.3), using the same backtest design (§4). This would give validation support to the practical use of cohort shocks (§7.2).

## 10.4 Simplicity of the financial demonstration and re-running it on a practical model

The fourth limitation is about the BEL sensitivity demonstration in §8. It is a demonstration with a simple projection model that uses benefit cash flows only and an independence approximation for lapses, and with 8 model points. For financial validation at a practical level, the following work remains. First, re-run it on a practical projection model that insurance companies already use in production. Then reconcile the results at the rate level and the present-value level, including measuring the actual run-time load. Second, validate the financial impact on a real in-force portfolio. This includes the policy distribution, premiums, expenses, and lapse behavior. Third, add the parts of the ESR implementation we did not apply. These are: integration across risk categories, eligible capital and the ESR ratio, the spread adjustment (the general bucket and similar items), and a more precise treatment of MOCE and required-capital integration, which §8.5 only approximated.

## 10.5 Stochastic extension

Finally, this framework is designed as a deterministic scenario generator. That is enough for the deterministic stresses that the standard regulatory method requires (§2.3). But a stochastic view helps with understanding the risk distribution in ORSA and with quantifying risk appetite. A natural direction is to add a stochastic layer on top of the validated deterministic core. One example is bootstrap generation of $L$ based on the distribution of improvement-rate residuals (§7.4). Integration with an Economic Scenario Generator (ESG) lies on the same line. This means building a systematic operation where disease-risk scenarios and economic-risk scenarios feed the same projection.

---

*None of these limitations overturns the central conclusion of this study — that explicit improvement-rate outputs with a validated direction work as a scenario generator. Instead, they define its scope of application and the path to refine it to a practical level. The next chapter states the conclusions of this paper.*
