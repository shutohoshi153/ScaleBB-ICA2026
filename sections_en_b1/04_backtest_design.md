# §4 Backtest Design

This chapter describes the design of the backtest. The backtest uses the model and the evaluation metrics defined in §3. The central design choice is the "3-cutoff design". We set the training cutoff year at three points: 2014, 2021, and 2022. This lets us separate two cases. In one case, COVID-19 (the structural break of 2020–2022) reaches the model as an unknown disturbance. In the other case, the model receives it as a learned shock. This design is the base for reading both the point forecast accuracy results (§5) and the directional accuracy findings (§6), including how they depend on the training period. We follow the notation of §3. The calendar year is $y$, and the cutoff year is $y_c$.

---

## 4.1 Validation framework — fit, project, validate

For each cutoff year $y_c$, we take the following steps.

1. Fit: We fit Scale BB-D (§3.2) and the 3 baselines (§3.4.1) to the cause-specific mortality rates $m(x, y)$ from the first observation year $y_0 = 1950$ to $y_c$. All methods use the same training period. This makes the comparison between methods fair.
2. Project: Starting from the last observed year $y_c$ ($= y_{\text{obs}}$), we generate future rates $\hat{m}(x, y)$ up to the end of the validation period $T = 2024$ (Equations 3.5–3.6).
3. Validate: We compare the forecasts for the hold-out interval $y \in [y_c + 1,\; T]$ with the actual data. We compute the metrics of §3.4.2 (MAPE, bias, and directional accuracy DA) for each cell of age × validation year.

The validation covers 8 diseases × 3 sexes. Ages run from 20 to 89, in 14 five-year age groups. The training period is a mixed series with unequal spacing: 5-year steps (1950–2010) and yearly steps (2013–$y_c$). However, the improvement rates stay consistent because Equation (3.3) converts them to annual rates.

Note that the convergence year of Scale BB-D is $P = 2035$ (§3.2.3). This year lies after every validation interval. So for every cutoff, the validation interval $[y_c+1, 2024]$ sits fully inside the blending interval $y_{\text{obs}} < y < P$. Therefore, the forecasts in the validation interval reflect the linear blend of the improvement rate at the last observed year $i(x, y_c)$ and the long-term rate $L_{\text{age}}(x)$ (Equation 3.5, the region where $0 < h(y) < 1$).

## 4.2 The 3-cutoff design and its purpose

We set the training cutoff at three points. In this way, we switch step by step whether the pandemic period is part of the model's training or not.

Table 4.1: The 3-cutoff design — training period, validation period, and how COVID-19 enters each cutoff

| cutoff $y_c$ | Training period | Training observation years | Validation period | Forecast horizon | How COVID-19 enters |
|---|---|---:|---|---:|---|
| 2014 | 1950–2014 | 15 | 2015–2024 | 10 years ahead | Unknown disturbance (the pandemic is not in the training data) |
| 2021 | 1950–2021 | 22 | 2022–2024 | 3 years ahead | Learned shock (the pandemic period is in the training data) |
| 2022 | 1950–2022 | 23 | 2023–2024 | 2 years ahead | Includes 1 post-pandemic year; uses the most recent data as much as possible |

![](figures/fig_4_1_backtest_design.png)

Figure 4.1: Diagram of the 3-cutoff design. Each row shows one cutoff. Blue is the training period (1950–$y_c$, "train window" in the figure). Red is the validation period ($y_c+1$–2024, "validation window" in the figure). The shaded area is the COVID-19 structural break period (2020–2022). For cutoff = 2014, the break falls in the validation period (unknown disturbance). For cutoff = 2021 / 2022, the break falls in the training period (learned shock). (Generated by: `reproduction/backtest/make_paper_figures.py`)

The three cutoffs have the following roles.

- cutoff = 2014 — COVID enters as an unknown disturbance. Training stops at 2014, and the model forecasts 10 years ahead, 2015–2024. The excess mortality of 2020–2022 is completely unknown to the model. This is the hardest test of how fragile a trend-extrapolation model is against a structural break. The validation interval is the largest (10 years × 14 ages), so the estimate of directional accuracy is the most stable in a statistical sense (§4.3).
- cutoff = 2021 — COVID enters as a learned shock. The training data includes the pandemic period (2020–2021), and the model forecasts the stable post-COVID period (2022–2024). This measures how much the point forecast recovers when the structural change of the pandemic is part of the training (the MAPE reduction in §5).
- cutoff = 2022 — Use the most recent data as much as possible. Training includes the post-pandemic year 2022, and the model forecasts 2023–2024. This checks the upper limit of point forecast performance when the training period is as long as possible.

These three points are not a continuous rolling-origin scheme (moving the origin one step at a time). They are three fixed origins, chosen on purpose so that they sit around the structural break of the pandemic. We place the cutoffs "just before the break (2014)", "at the end of the break (2021)", and "after the break (2022)". In this way, we can read the model's response to the same structural break as a function of how much of the break is in the training data.

## 4.3 Number of validation cells and statistical meaning

The number of validation cells for each cutoff comes from 8 diseases × 3 sexes × 14 age groups × the number of validation years.

$$
N_{\text{cell}} = \underbrace{8}_{\text{diseases}} \times \underbrace{3}_{\text{sexes}} \times \underbrace{14}_{\text{age groups}} \times \underbrace{H}_{\text{validation years}}
$$

Table 4.2: Number of validation cells by cutoff

| cutoff | Validation years $H$ | Validation cells $N_{\text{cell}}$ |
|---|---:|---:|
| 2014 | 10 | 3,360 |
| 2021 | 3 | 1,008 |
| 2022 | 2 | 672 |

cutoff = 2014 has the largest number of cells. So its aggregate values by disease and by age are the most stable. In contrast, cutoff = 2021 / 2022 have short validation periods and few cells. Because of this, the metrics can vary a lot between diseases, and readers should keep this in mind. (This is why, in §6, the directional accuracy for cutoff = 2021 swings widely from disease to disease.)

Note that the directional accuracy DA (Equations 3.11–3.12) is evaluated only on the cells where an actual change happened, $\mathcal{D} = \{(x,y): \Delta_{\text{act}} \neq 0\}$. So its effective number of cells is smaller than $N_{\text{cell}}$ in Table 4.2, and it differs by cutoff and by disease. The tables in §6 state the effective cell counts for each aggregate.

## 4.4 What the backtest measures (applying §3.4)

For each cutoff, we apply the two groups of metrics defined in §3.4.2.

- Point forecast accuracy (reported in §5): MAPE (Equation 3.9) and bias (Equation 3.10). These measure how close the forecast level is to the actual data.
- Directional accuracy (reported in §6): DA (Equation 3.12). This measures whether the model can predict if the rate moves up or down compared with the last observed year $y_c$.

We run Scale BB-D and the 3 baselines on the same 3-cutoff design and compare both groups of metrics. In this way, we quantify the gap "the model performs worse in point forecasts but predicts the direction correctly" (the turn from §5 to §6), including how it depends on the training period.

## 4.5 Reproduction

The script `run_all.sh` in the reproduction package `reproduction/backtest/` runs the full 3-cutoff design of this chapter in one go. The results for cutoff = 2014 go directly under `output/`. The results for cutoff = 2021 / 2022 go to `output/cutoff_2021/` and `output/cutoff_2022/`. Then `compare_cutoffs.py` produces the cross-cutoff comparison tables (matching the tables of §5.2–5.3 and Figures 5.4–5.5). The training and validation periods of each cutoff are set explicitly by the CLI arguments `--train-cutoff` / `--validation-end`. The full reproduction steps, the expected key figures, and the modifications made to the original scripts are documented in `reproduction/backtest/README.md`.
