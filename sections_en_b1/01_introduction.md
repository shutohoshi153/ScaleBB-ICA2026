# §1 Introduction

This paper extends Scale BB, the mortality improvement scale that the Society of Actuaries (SOA) published in 2012. We extend it from all-cause mortality to cause-specific (disease-specific) mortality. We test the extension with a 10-year backtest. After this test, we position the model as a scenario generator. Such a generator is required by economic-value-based valuation of insurance liabilities. Examples are the Insurance Capital Standard (ICS), the international accounting standard IFRS 17, and, in Japan, the economic-value-based solvency regulation (ESR). This chapter explains three things. First, the two changes in the environment that created this problem setting (§1.1). Second, the approach and the novelty of this study (§1.2). Third, the structure of the findings that runs through the paper, and the plan of each chapter (§1.3). A note on notation: we write Scale BB for the original SOA scale. We write Scale BB-D for the extended framework of this study (D stands for Disease, the extension to disease-specific rates).

---

## 1.1 Background — Two Turning Points

Structural change in disease rates. The COVID-19 pandemic was the largest shock to mortality and life expectancy in recent years. Its effect was not limited to the direct excess mortality in 2020–2022. It also reached the trends of cause-specific mortality and incidence rates. This happened through changes in care-seeking behavior, pressure on the healthcare system, and long-term health effects. OECD (2023) pointed out that the pandemic made mortality improvement assumptions unstable. It saw the main part of the shock as temporary, but noted large uncertainty about long-term health effects. It said that future improvement-rate models must be transparent and consistent with future expectations. A practice note by the American Academy of Actuaries (AAA) also gives guidance for setting assumptions after an external shock. It stresses the choice between static tables and generational tables, the adjustment of long-term improvement rates and the move from short-term to long-term rates, and the importance of sensitivity analysis and documented judgment. In fact, our own backtest shows the same problem. The validation MAPE (Mean Absolute Percentage Error) of the same model changes by 6 to 8 times, depending on whether the COVID-19 shock is included in training (§5.3). Assumption practice in third-sector insurance (medical and disease coverage) has relied on static assumed rate tables. In this situation, the danger of the assumption that "past experience rates simply continue" became visible.

The turn in Japan's capital regulation environment. In the same period, the valuation framework for insurance liabilities and capital is completing its move to an economic-value basis. The EU's Solvency II applied from 2016. The international accounting standard IFRS 17 took effect in 2023. Then the Insurance Capital Standard (ICS) was adopted in December 2024. In Japan, the economic-value-based solvency regulation (ESR) started to apply from fiscal year 2025. This paper is being written exactly in the year when ICS takes effect and ESR applies. These frameworks share one point. The calculation of liabilities and capital is not a single best-estimate point forecast. It consists of a base scenario, a set of prescribed stress scenarios (level, trend, catastrophe), sensitivity disclosure, and ORSA (Own Risk and Solvency Assessment) stress (details in §2.3).

The gap on the disease-risk axis. At the crossing point of these two turns, there is a gap in practice. On the economic-risk axis, a systematic scenario supply tool exists: the Economic Scenario Generator (ESG). On the disease-risk axis, however, the main practice is to multiply a static assumed rate table by a uniform factor. This method cannot express a trend shock — a change with a time structure, expressed as an improvement rate. In other words, what practice needs now is not "a single point forecast with better accuracy". It needs "a mechanism that generates several scenarios from the same base while keeping internal consistency". This is the problem setting of this paper.

## 1.2 Approach and Novelty of This Study

Approach. Scale BB is a practical scale with a small number of parameters that are easy to interpret (§2.1). It smooths all-cause mortality experience in two dimensions, age by calendar year, and extracts improvement rates. It then blends the improvement rates of recent experience toward a long-term assumed improvement rate $L$ by a convergence year $P$. This study extends this framework so that it can be applied to cause-specific mortality (§3.2–3.3). As input, we use cause-specific mortality from the Vital Statistics of Japan (MHLW) (1950–2024, 8 diseases x 3 sexes x 21 age groups). We test the model with a backtest that uses three different training cutoff years (§4). We check both point forecast accuracy and directional accuracy.

The novelty has two layers. First (Novelty A), the extension itself: applying a Scale BB type improvement scale to cause-specific mortality. The original Scale BB and its successors (the MP series and the CMI model) all target all-cause mortality only. As far as the authors know, no one has presented a systematic extension that separates and extracts, in one framework, the improvement trends, period shocks, and cohort effects that differ by cause of death (§2.1–2.2). Second (Novelty B), direct application to death benefits contingent on specific diseases. Some products pay benefits when death is caused by a specific disease. Examples are death coverage for the three major diseases, cancer death insurance, and disease-specific death riders by cause of death. For these products, the cause-specific mortality tested in this study is the assumption itself. The scenario generator can be applied directly. For disease incidence rates in medical insurance, the situation is different. Public statistics do not contain a long-term panel of incidence rates. So cause-specific mortality works as a proxy for validating the methodology (§3.1.3). This two-layer distinction — proxy and direct — is a premise that runs through the whole paper. Also, forecasting mortality after diagnosis (the case fatality rate) is outside the scope of this study. We make no claim about it in any form (§3.1.3, §10).

## 1.3 Structure of the Findings and Plan of the Paper

The structure of this paper is not a simple success story in which the original test plan worked as planned. It has a three-step structure: a negative conclusion, then an unexpected discovery, and finally new insight. We show the skeleton first.

Original goal and negative conclusion (§5). The original goal was to test whether the Scale BB-D extension is useful for point forecasts of disease rates. The answer is clearly no. Scale BB-D performs worse in MAPE than the best simple baseline in all cutoffs and all 24 cells (8 diseases x 3 sexes). With training cutoff 2014, the gap is +16 to +44pp.

Unexpected discovery (§6). However, the picture changes completely when we measure the same model output by the "direction of change". The directional accuracy for the six main diseases reaches 77.7–95.0%. This is far above the baseline methods, which carry direction information either not at all or only by chance. The model misses the point forecast but gets the direction right. This gap is not an accident. It follows from the design, which respects the long-term trend and deliberately ignores the noise at the end of the observation period (§6.4).

Translation into new insight (§7–§9). As §2.3 explains, what economic-value-based valuation mainly requires is not point forecast accuracy. It requires internal consistency between scenarios. The basic condition for this is that the direction of the improvement rates produced by the model has been verified in advance. From this viewpoint, §7 repositions Scale BB-D as a scenario generator. §8 quantifies the practical significance with a sensitivity demonstration of BEL (Best Estimate Liability). This demonstration follows the ESR implementation details (the risk-free interest rate curve and the stress factors set by regulatory notice). One contrast stands out (§8.4). The effect of the uniform level shock in the regulation is almost constant across entry ages (+17 to 19%). In contrast, the trend shock has an age gradient. For young entry ages, it reaches about 1.7 times the regulatory shock. This shows an age structure of risk that current practice with uniform factors cannot capture. §9 discusses calibration guidance for adoption and the adoption workload.

In one sentence, the claim of this paper is as follows. The Scale BB-D extension to cause-specific mortality performs worse than simple baselines in short-term point forecasts. But it explicitly outputs improvement rates $i^*(x, y)$ whose direction has been verified by backtest. So it has a structural advantage as the scenario generator that economic-value-based valuation requires. And cause-specific mortality plays two roles. For disease incidence rates in medical insurance, it is a proxy for validating the methodology. For death benefits contingent on specific diseases, it is the target itself. In the latter case, it can be applied directly, without the limits of a proxy.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. §2 covers prior research and regulatory requirements. §3 covers data and methods. §4 covers the test design. §5 gives the results on point forecast accuracy. §6 presents the discovery about directional accuracy. §7 repositions the model as a scenario generator. §8 demonstrates the financial impact. §9 gives practical guidelines. §10 discusses limitations and future work. §11 concludes.
