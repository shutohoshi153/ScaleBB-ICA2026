# §2 Related Work and Regulatory Requirements

This chapter organizes two things. First, the prior research that is the starting point of this study. Second, the regulatory requirements that define the evaluation axes of this study. In §2.1 we review the design ideas of the original SOA (2012) *Mortality Improvement Scale BB*. The original work targets all-cause mortality. We contrast this with the cause-specific extension of this study (Novelty A). In §2.2 we review prior research on APC (Age-Period-Cohort) models and disease-rate modeling. We identify the gap that this study fills. In §2.3 we organize the scenario structure that ICS, IFRS 17, and Solvency II require for the valuation of insurance liabilities. We place the regulatory requirements here, before the main part (§5 and later), for a clear reason. The evaluation axes of this study do not end with "point forecast accuracy". What regulation mainly requires is not a single point forecast. It requires several scenarios with internal consistency. We want to confirm this before we start to interpret the results.

---

## 2.1 SOA (2012) Scale BB and the Design Idea of the 2D-to-1D Reduction

Scale BB is an alternative scale that the Society of Actuaries (SOA) presented in 2012. It was a response to the limits of the older one-dimensional mortality improvement scale, Scale AA. Its design has the following three elements.

1. Capturing the improvement structure in two dimensions. The scale takes the all-cause mortality experience of the U.S. Social Security Administration (SSA). It applies P-spline smoothing in two dimensions (2D), age by calendar year. It extracts the improvement rates as a surface. On the heat map, vertical patterns show period effects (example: influenza epidemics). Diagonal patterns show cohort effects (example: differences in smoking habits between generations). The idea of identifying these patterns and bringing period shocks into the model was made explicit here.
2. Blending toward a long-term improvement rate. The scale does not simply extrapolate the most recent observed improvement rates. Instead, it moves linearly from a fixed starting point (the year 2005) to a long-term assumed improvement rate of 1.0% per year at the convergence year (the cohort convergence period is 10 years; the blend function $h(y)$ in §7.4 of the original paper). This structure connects the recent trend and the long-term assumption in an explicit way. It follows the same idea as the UK CMI Mortality Projections Model, which blends from recently estimated improvement rates to a long-term assumed improvement rate.
3. A 1D reduction for compatibility with practice. The 2D information is reduced to a one-dimensional (1D) scale by age. The reduction is made so that the financial impact is approximately the same. This lets users apply the scale without changing their valuation software. Scale BB called itself an "interim scale", but it was still widely used. A large reason is this design, which lowers the friction of adoption in practice.

Later, the SOA moved to a new system with the successor MP-2014 and its updates. In that system, a two-dimensional scale by age and calendar year is updated every year. So the framework of capturing the improvement structure in 2D has itself become the standard in U.S. practice.

Contrast with this study (Novelty A). The original Scale BB and its successor scales all target all-cause mortality. For cause-specific (disease-specific) mortality, each cause has its own improvement trend, period shocks, and cohort effects. As far as the author knows, no systematic extension *within the improvement-scale family* has been presented that separates and extracts these inside one common framework. (Cause-specific mortality has of course been forecast by other families of methods; we discuss that literature and the exact boundary of our claim in §2.2.) This study makes this extension (§3.2–3.3). It then connects the output to the scenario generation required by the regulations described in §2.3 (§7). The extended framework has a double applicability. It can serve as a proxy for method validation for disease incidence rates in medical insurance. It can also serve as a direct assumption for death benefits contingent on specific diseases. We define this in §3.1.3.

## 2.2 Prior Research on APC Models and Disease-Rate Modeling

Two lines of mortality modeling. There are, broadly, two lines of research for forecasting future mortality. The first line is stochastic models. It started with the log-bilinear model of Lee and Carter (1992). It was extended with a cohort term (Renshaw and Haberman 2006). The CBD model focused on old-age mortality (Cairns, Blake and Dowd 2006). Smoothing and extrapolation with P-splines followed (Currie, Durban and Eilers 2004). These models give future mortality as a probability distribution. The second line is practical scales. Examples are the Scale AA / Scale BB / MP series (U.S.) and the CMI model (UK). They are given as deterministic tables of improvement rates. They have a small number of parameters that people can interpret (long-term improvement rate, convergence period). They focus on a form that valuation practice can use directly. This study stands in the second line. It extends that all-cause framework to cause-specific rates. The two lines share the work in a clear way: stochastic models give distributions, and practical scales give deterministic scenarios. This matches the fact that the regulatory requirements in §2.3 are based on deterministic stresses. (A stochastic extension is a future task in §10.)

APC models. The decomposition into three effects — age, period, and cohort — was formalized in epidemiology by Holford (1983), Clayton and Schifflers (1987), and others. There is a rich body of work, mainly on descriptive analysis of cancer incidence and mortality. The identification problem comes from the linear dependence of the three effects ($\text{age} + \text{cohort} = \text{period}$). Its solution through constraints was also organized in this line of research. The APC extension of this study (§3.3) follows Holford's convention (reference-cell constraints, and moving the linear component into the period effect). However, the main goal of epidemiological APC research is to describe the past and to attribute causes. It does not provide a framework that turns the output into the assumption format of insurance practice — rate tables by age and calendar year with improvement scales, and future projections that valuation systems can consume.

Forecasting of cause-specific mortality. A third line of research forecasts mortality by cause of death directly. Wilmoth (1995) already showed the central difficulty: when each cause is extrapolated on its own, the slowly declining causes come to dominate the total, so the aggregate forecast is pessimistic relative to a forecast made on all-cause mortality. Later work responded to this in two ways. One line models the dependence between causes explicitly, so that the trends of the individual causes stay coherent with each other and with the all-cause trend (for example, Alai, Arnold and Sherris 2015, and the related work of Arnold and Sherris on cause-of-death trend interaction). Another line treats cause-specific death distributions as compositional data. Modelling the distribution of deaths across causes rather than the rates themselves keeps the cause shares summing to one by construction and lets the dependence between causes enter the model directly, which yields coherent cause-specific projections (Kjærgaard, Ergemen, Kallestrup-Lamb, Oeppen and Lindahl-Jacobsen 2019). This body of work is directly relevant to our setting, and we do not claim priority over it.

What separates this study from that line is the *output format*, not the act of forecasting by cause. Those methods are stochastic or compositional forecasting models: their deliverable is a projected rate series (or a distribution of it) for each cause. They do not deliver a small set of interpretable improvement-scale parameters — a long-term improvement rate $L$, a convergence year $P$, an age taper — that valuation practice can hold as an assumption and vary as a scenario. Nor do they validate the *sign* of the improvement rate as the property on which scenario consistency rests (§2.3). Our extension therefore sits alongside that literature rather than replacing it: it takes the practical-scale form of the second line above and applies it to causes of death, so that the output can be consumed by the regulatory scenario structure of §2.3. Correspondingly, the claim of Novelty A in this paper is narrow, and should be read as: *no improvement-scale-type framework in the Scale BB / MP / CMI family has been extended to cause-specific mortality with the improvement trend, period shock and cohort effect of each cause separated inside one common framework.* We make no claim that cause-specific mortality forecasting itself is new.

Projection of disease rates. At the international level, large epidemiological projects such as the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) provide long-term estimates of disease-specific incidence and mortality. But their goal is to estimate the public health burden. They do not have the granularity and format that the valuation of insurance liabilities needs: consistency of age-specific rates as a surface, and a parameterization that allows scenario operations. On the insurance side, the development of improvement scales for incidence rates (morbidity) is far behind mortality. In Japan, one reason is a limit of official statistics: the Patient Survey (MHLW) runs on a 3-year cycle, so a long-term panel cannot be formed (§3.1). In Japanese practice, the Standard Mortality Tables and each company's experience rate tables are static tables. For disease rates, no officially maintained improvement scale exists.

The gap this study fills. In short: (i) prior research on improvement scales is limited to all-cause mortality; (ii) cause-specific and disease-specific APC analysis stays at epidemiological description and is not provided in a practical assumption format; (iii) the cause-specific forecasting literature does produce projections by cause, but as rate series or distributions rather than as an interpretable improvement scale that can be varied as a scenario; (iv) none of these lines presents the connection in which the direction of the improvement-rate output is first validated by backtest and then used to generate the scenarios that regulation requires. This study fills the crossing point of these four: the Scale BB extension to cause-specific rates (Novelty A), output in a practice-compatible format, and use as a scenario generator based on directional validation (§6–§7).

## 2.3 The Scenario Structure Required by Economic-Value-Based Regulation (ICS / IFRS 17 / Solvency II)

A shift in the regulatory environment. The frameworks for valuing insurance liabilities and capital are in a period of transition. They are moving from static valuation under lock-in assumptions to an economic-value-based approach. In the EU, Solvency II was applied in 2016. In international accounting standards, IFRS 17 became effective in 2023. The IAIS adopted the Insurance Capital Standard (ICS) in December 2024. In Japan, the economic-value-based solvency regulation (ESR) applies from fiscal year 2025, so its first valuation date is 31 March 2026. The time of writing of this paper is exactly the year when ICS becomes effective and ESR first applies (§1).

These frameworks share one common point. The calculation of liabilities and capital is not built from "a single best estimate point forecast". It is built from "a base scenario plus a set of prescribed stress scenarios". The required scenarios can be organized into roughly three types.

- Level shock: an immediate, uniform change in the rate level (for example, a uniform increase or decrease of mortality)
- Trend shock: a change in the future improvement (or worsening) trend — an intervention in the time structure of the rates themselves
- Catastrophe shock: a temporary excess placed on one specific year only (for example, a pandemic)

Trend in the base scenario itself. It would be a mistake to read the trend axis as belonging only to the stress side. Japan's ESR contemplates it inside the current estimate as well. The Q&A that accompanies the Pillar 1 notice states that whether to reflect a forward-looking trend in insurance assumptions such as mortality rates, to what degree, and over what period, is for each company to judge in the light of its own situation — and that the objectivity of that judgment must be secured, for example by confirming consistency with public data such as future demographic projections and with the product characteristics of the underwriting conditions and benefits. The same Q&A requires the assumptions of the current estimate to reflect expected future developments in demographics, law, medicine, technology, society and the economy, including risks that the past data do not yet exhibit but that are expected to arise.

This matters for what follows in two ways. First, a mechanism that produces improvement rates from public statistics, in a form whose direction can be checked against those statistics, is not merely convenient for stress testing: it is a way of discharging the objectivity requirement that attaches to the base assumption itself. Second, the same Q&A asks that the effect of the principal assumptions on the current estimate be understood through sensitivity analysis. The demonstration in §8 is exactly that exercise.

On top of these, there is sensitivity disclosure (IFRS 17: disclosure of the change in liabilities when key insurance risk assumptions move within a reasonable range). There are also ORSA and medium- to long-term stress tests (a quantitative translation of narrative scenarios). The table below organizes the concrete calculations and the rate-scenario inputs they need.

Table 2.1: Calculations required by economic-value-based regulation and the rate-scenario input each needs

| Calculation | Required rate-scenario input |
|---|---|
| BEL (Best Estimate Liability) | Future rates of the base scenario $m(x, y)$ |
| Risk adjustment (IFRS 17) / risk margin | Future rates stressed in the unfavorable direction |
| ICS capital charge for insurance risk | Future rates with level shock and trend shock applied |
| Catastrophe risk | Future rates with an excess shock placed on specific years only |
| Sensitivity disclosure (IFRS 17) | Future rates with only one single assumption moved, and the resulting change in liabilities |
| ORSA / medium- to long-term stress | Future rates that translate narrative scenarios, such as worsening public health or a slowdown of medical progress |

Internal consistency between scenarios. Each calculation in Table 2.1 is not an independent forecasting problem. It is a problem of generating a set of scenarios that start from the same base. Therefore the following properties are required.

1. All scenarios must keep the same age-curve shape (smoothness of the rate surface).
2. Only the rate level and the trend may move, while the relative relations between ages are kept.
3. It must be possible to put separate shocks on the short term and the long term (example: a trend shock for 5 years only, then normal afterwards).
4. When many scenarios run through the same calculation flow, the results must be comparable with each other.

If these break down, the difference between base and stress becomes a mix of two things: the effect of the stress itself, and the calibration error of the model. Then it cannot be used as a basis for regulatory capital calculation or disclosure. In particular, suppose the sign of the trend that the model outputs (improvement or worsening) is wrong in the first place. Then the operation of adding a trend shock loses its meaning. In other words, prior validation of the direction of the improvement rates is a basic condition for a scenario generator (we measure this in §6 and discuss it in §7).

A gap in current practice. In current assumption practice for disease incidence rates (and cause-specific mortality), the main method is to multiply a static assumed rate table by a uniform safety loading factor. This method can express a level shock. But it cannot explicitly express a trend shock — a change with the time structure of an improvement rate. On the economic-risk axis, an Economic Scenario Generator (ESG) provides a systematic supply of scenarios. On the disease-risk axis, no corresponding scenario-generation mechanism exists today. (We discuss this contrast in detail in §7.)

Implication of this chapter. Once we confirm this structure of requirements first, we see that the validation in this study should be read along two axes. The first axis is "does the point forecast hit the target". As we report in §5, on this axis our framework consistently performs worse than simple baselines. The second axis is "can it generate the scenario structure of Table 2.1 with internal consistency". This is what regulation mainly requires. The process of turning the negative result on the first axis into a question on the second axis forms the narrative structure of this paper from §5 to §6–§7.
