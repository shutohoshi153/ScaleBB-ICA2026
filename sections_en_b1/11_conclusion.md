# §11 Conclusion

This paper extended Scale BB, the mortality improvement scale of the SOA (2012), from all-cause mortality to cause-specific (disease-specific) mortality. We tested it with a 3-cutoff backtest on a panel from the Vital Statistics of Japan (MHLW) for 1950–2024. The results form three steps. First, the original goal failed: the model is not useful for point forecasts. Scale BB-D performs worse in MAPE than the best simple baseline at every cutoff and in all 24 disease × sex cells (+16 to +44pp at $y_c = 2014$, §5). Second, we measured the same output again by the direction of change. The directional accuracy for the six main diseases reaches 77.7–95.0%. This shows a clear gap from the baselines, which hold direction information only by chance or not at all in their structure (§6). Third, we can translate this gap into the language of the scenario structure that economic-value-based valuation (ICS / IFRS 17, and ESR in Japan) requires. The explicit output of the improvement rate $i^*(x, y)$, whose direction was validated in advance, can generate level, trend, and catastrophe shocks, sensitivity disclosures, and ORSA stress in an internally consistent way. It only needs setting changes that share the same fit (§7). The baseline methods that win on point forecasts cannot play this role because of their structure (§7.3). In short, the true value of Scale BB-D is not a replacement for point forecasts. It is a scenario generator.

The BEL sensitivity demonstration in §8 gave numbers for the practical meaning of this repositioning. The calculation followed the ESR implementation details (the risk-free interest rate curve and the Pillar 1 stress factors set by regulatory notice). Under the uniform level shock in the regulation (incidence rate +20%), the effect on the present value of benefits is almost constant across entry ages (+17.2 to 19.1%). In contrast, the trend shock (improvement rate set to zero) has a clear age gradient: +31 to 32% for entry age 30 and +10 to 11% for entry age 60. For young policies, it reaches about 1.7 times the regulatory level shock. The uncertainty of the improvement trend concentrates in young, long-term policies. The current practice of a static table with uniform factors cannot express this age structure of the risk in principle. The demonstration shows, in the vocabulary of the regulation, a direct link: whether an assumption base can handle trend scenarios decides the quality of risk understanding for a portfolio.

The contribution of this study has two layers. As Novelty A, we extended a Scale BB type improvement scale to cause-specific mortality. As far as the author knows, this is the first framework *in the improvement-scale family* (Scale BB / MP / CMI) that separates and extracts improvement trends, period shocks, and cohort effects for each cause of death inside one common framework. We do not claim that cause-specific mortality forecasting is itself new (§2.2). As Novelty B, we showed that this output becomes a direct assumption, without any proxy, for death benefits contingent on specific diseases — products where cause-specific death is the benefit trigger. So we set up two layers of application targets: medical insurance (proxy application to incidence rates) and death benefits contingent on specific diseases (direct application). For the former, the direction validation in §6 works as validation of the methodology. For the latter, it works as validation of the assumption itself.

This year, ICS came into force and ESR started to apply in Japan. It is a turning point: assumption practice for disease risk must move from "a single point forecast" to "a consistent set of scenarios". This study showed that the tool that meets this demand is not a new predictor that wins the accuracy race. The right tool is a framework that first fixes, as its base, a property we can test even with limited data — the direction of improvement rates. It then turns long-term assumptions, which tests cannot reach, into scenarios through explicit parameters that people can interpret. The extension of a "provisional scale", which loses to simple methods in point forecasts, has a structural advantage in the era of economic-value-based valuation. This paradox is the new finding that this paper found beyond the negative conclusion of the backtest.
